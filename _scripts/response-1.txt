Investment certificates promise high returns with capital protection, but they embed hidden costs, barrier risks, and limited liquidity that many investors overlook.

## What investment certificates are  
Issued by banks as unsecured debt securities, certificates combine a bond component (repaying capital at maturity) with one or more options that determine upside participation and any conditional protection. The most common retail product in Italy in 2026 is the conditional‑capital‑protection certificate, which guarantees full repayment only if the underlying stays above a preset barrier (typically 60‑70% of the initial value).

## The main risks  
- **Implicit cost:** Banks embed a margin of 2‑5% per annum in the product price, reducing the effective participation in the underlying’s upside.  
- **Barrier risk:** If the barrier is breached, the “protection” disappears and investors can lose a large portion of capital, exactly when markets are most stressed. Historical crashes have seen equity declines of 35‑55%, making barrier breaches more likely than marketing suggests.  
- **Liquidity:** Secondary‑market trading is dominated by the issuer, with bid‑ask spreads of 1‑3% and prices often below fair value, especially during market turmoil.  

## When a certificate might be justified  
Certificates can be useful for investors who need to offset capital losses in Italy’s fiscal carry‑forward basket, as gains are taxed as “redditi diversi” and can offset prior losses. They also appeal to those who prefer a single, pre‑structured product rather than managing separate bonds and ETFs, or to ultra‑risk‑averse clients seeking 100% capital‑protected exposure (subject to the bank’s credit risk). In all other cases, the opaque costs, barrier exposure, and liquidity constraints make alternative investments such as a bond‑plus‑ETF combination a more transparent and efficient choice.

## Opaque pricing and embedded margins  
Certificates are structured debt securities that combine a bond with one or more options. The issuer purchases these components on the wholesale market and sells the packaged product at a higher price, embedding a margin that typically ranges from **2 % to 5 % of the notional per year**. This cost is not disclosed in a TER or fee sheet, meaning investors receive a lower participation in the underlying’s upside than if they bought the bond and options directly.

## Barrier risk and limited liquidity  
The most common retail offering in Italy is the **conditional‑capital‑protection certificate**, which protects the principal only if the underlying stays above a barrier—often set at **60 %–70 % of the initial value**. If the barrier is breached, the protection disappears and investors can lose a large portion of their capital. Moreover, the secondary market is dominated by the issuing bank, resulting in **wide bid‑ask spreads of 1 %–3 %** and prices that usually fall below the theoretical fair value, especially during market stress.

## When (if ever) they might be justified  
Certificates can be useful for investors needing to offset capital losses in Italy’s “fiscal carry‑forward” basket, as gains are classified as *redditi diversi* and can be netted against losses. However, the tax benefit (up to 26 % of the loss recovered) often does not outweigh the implicit 3 %–5 % cost, and the product remains inferior to a transparent bond‑plus‑ETF combination on every other dimension. Investors should treat certificates as a niche tool, not a default “safe‑high‑yield” solution.
